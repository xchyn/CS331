Review · Lectures 1–12 Midterm

What to revise, and how

Eight problems, 100 points, 75 minutes, closed book. This page says what each problem asks for, how to go about it, and what loses marks — then gives you fresh practice for every question type that can be practised on paper. Nothing here is taken from the exam, and nothing here is taken from the problem sets, so working through it tells you something the sets cannot.

M.1What the exam looks like

Closed book. No notes, no computer, no phone, no calculator. Eight problems on 100 points, 75 minutes — which is 45 seconds a point, and worth knowing before you spend twenty minutes perfecting a diagram worth thirteen.

Problems 1, 2 and 3 stand on their own. Problems 4 to 8 all run against one database, printed on the last page of the exam — five or six tables with their primary keys underlined and their foreign keys listed underneath. You may tear that page off, and you should: you will be looking at it for roughly half the exam.

Where the points are

45 of the 100 points are queries (Problems 5 to 8), and another 20 are the algebra of Problem 3. Two thirds of the exam is relational algebra and SQL. The two drawing questions are 25 between them.

So if revision time is short, it goes on queries. If you can write the eight expressions of Problem 3 and the four queries of Problems 5 to 8, you have 65 points before you draw anything.

Three conventions that are marked

The join. Sets 5 to 8 avoid the join operator and write every match out with a product and a condition. Keep doing that — it is what the lecture notes do and it is never wrong. But note that Problem 3 may ask you whether R ⋈ S can be constructed, which is a question about the operator's definition, so you still have to know it.

SQL vocabulary. Everything on the exam can be written with what Lectures 11 and 12 give you: SELECT, FROM, WHERE with the comma product, DISTINCT, IN and NOT IN against a subquery, the five aggregate functions, GROUP BY, HAVING and ORDER BY. You will not need EXISTS, ALL, ANY or EXCEPT, and using them is not a safe bet under time pressure.

Show your work. The paper says so. On an algebra question that means naming your intermediate relations and writing the condition F out on its own line, the way Lecture 9 does. A wrong final expression with a readable derivation is worth more than a wrong one-liner.

M.2Problem 1 — the diagram [13 pts]

You are given four or five sentences describing an organisation and asked for the ER/EER diagram, with cardinality and participation marked. The description is written so that each sentence carries one or two modelling decisions; read it with that in mind and the diagram almost writes itself.

The method, in order

1. Underline every noun that has its own identifier. A sentence like “each X is identified by its Y” is telling you two things: X is an entity set, and Y is its key. Draw those boxes first.

2. Watch for an entity identified by two things. “Identified by the name and the type” means a composite key — both attributes underlined, not one. This appears nearly every time.

3. Watch for an entity identified by another entity. If a thing is identified by “the course, the instructor and the date”, it has no key of its own: it is either a weak entity set with a partial key, or a relationship among three entity sets. Decide which and say why.

4. Attributes that describe a pair go on the relationship. A score that depends on both the student and the session belongs on the diamond, not in either box. This is the single most common place to lose a point.

5. Subclasses last. “There are also X who ...” followed by an attribute only those X have is a specialization. Draw the circle, mark d or o, and mark whether it is total (double line) or partial (single).

6. Then walk every edge and write both ends. Not just the side you were thinking about.

What costs marks

A cardinality written at one end only. Every edge has two ends; both get a number or a letter.

Participation left off. Total participation is a double line. If the text says “every lesson must have an instructor”, that is a double line, and leaving it single is a different diagram.

A relationship attribute parked on an entity. Ask: if I deleted one of the two participants, would this value still mean anything? If not, it belongs on the relationship.

A subclass drawn as a separate unrelated box. It needs the circle and the subset arcs, or it is not a specialization.

Inventing an identifier. If the description does not give an entity a key, that is deliberate — it is a weak entity set, and adding a made-up ID throws away the point the question was testing.

Where to practise. Set 1 has sixteen full diagrams to draw and Set 2 has eight more with specialization in them. Exercises 1.14 to 1.19 and 2.6 to 2.9 are the closest in size to a 13-point exam question; the longer ones in Set 1 are larger than anything you will be asked for in 75 minutes. Work them with the solution closed, then compare edge by edge rather than overall — it is the ends of the edges that differ.

M.3Problem 2 — diagram to schemas [12 pts]

An EER diagram is given; you write the relational schemas and identify every primary key and every foreign key. This is the most mechanical question on the paper, which cuts both ways: there is a procedure that always works, and there is no partial credit for arriving somewhere sensible by another route.

In the diagramIn the schemas
Strong entity setIts own relation; its key underlined
Weak entity setIts own relation; key is the owner's key plus the partial key; the owner's key is also a foreign key
Composite attributeOne column per leaf; the composite itself disappears
Multivalued attributeA relation of its own: the owner's key plus the value, both in the primary key
Derived attributeNothing — it is computed, not stored
1 : M relationshipThe key of the one side becomes a foreign key on the many side. No new relation
1 : M with an attributeSame, and the attribute rides along on the many side
M : N relationshipA new relation: both keys, both foreign, both in the primary key; any relationship attribute joins them
1 : 1 relationshipA foreign key on either side — put it on the side with total participation
SpecializationOne relation per subclass, keyed by the superclass key, which is also a foreign key
What costs marks

Forgetting that the weak entity's foreign key is also part of its primary key. It is both. Write it twice.

Putting the foreign key on the wrong side of a 1 : M. It goes on the many side, always. Putting it on the one side would need a multivalued column, which the relational model does not have.

Listing primary keys and forgetting foreign keys. The question asks for both and the marks are split. Go through your schemas a second time looking only for foreign keys.

Creating a relation for a 1 : M relationship. It is not wrong as a design, but it is not what the mapping algorithm produces, and the question is about the algorithm.

Where to practise. Set 3 is exactly this question, twelve times. Exercises 3.1 to 3.4 are the mapping drills; 3.10 to 3.12 map the diagrams you drew in Sets 1 and 2, which is the closest thing on the site to sitting the exam question cold.

M.4Problem 3 — keys and legal operations [20 pts]

The biggest single question on the paper, and the most mechanical once you know the rules. Four small relations are printed with their contents. Two parts ask about keys; six ask you to construct an expression or explain why it is impossible. That last clause is the whole question: some of the six cannot be built, and saying clearly why earns the same marks as computing one that can.

When is an operation impossible?

There is only one rule, and it governs three operators.

∪, ∩ and − need union-compatible operands. Two relations are union-compatible when they have the same number of attributes and corresponding attributes are drawn from the same domains. The exam tells you how to read domains: two domains are identical if and only if they have identical name. So compare the attribute names. Different arity, or the same arity with a name that does not line up, and the operation is impossible — say which pair fails and stop.

σ, Π and × are almost never impossible. Selection needs its condition to mention only attributes the relation actually has, and to compare things of the same domain. Projection needs the attributes to exist. The product needs the two relations to have no attribute name in common, or the result would have two columns with one name.

The natural join is never impossible, but it is sometimes not what you expect. With common attributes it matches on all of them; with no common attributes it degenerates into the product. Say which case you are in.

Keys: what an instance can and cannot tell you

An instance can disprove a key and can never prove one. Find two rows that agree on the attributes in question and you have settled it: not a superkey, and the two rows are your proof. Find none and all you may say is that nothing in this instance forbids it — a key is a constraint the designer declares about every possible state, not a pattern you read off five rows.

Write both halves. “No, because of these two tuples” is a complete answer; “yes” on its own is not.

And remember the difference between the two words. A superkey identifies a row. A candidate key is a superkey with nothing to spare: remove any attribute and it stops identifying. So showing a set is a superkey is only half of showing it is a candidate key.

The marking, part by part

Six constructions and two key questions on 20 points is roughly 2½ points each. An impossible one takes fifteen seconds once you have checked the arity; a product takes as long as it takes to write the rows out. Do them in the order they are cheapest, not in the order they are printed.

M.6Problem 4 — which constraint breaks [10 pts]

Four short parts. Each gives you a sequence of inserts against an empty database and asks whether it succeeds — and if not, which constraint stops it. The answer is always one of five, and naming the right one is where the marks are.

ConstraintSaysBreaks when
D.C. domaineach value comes from its column's domaina number where a date belongs, a string too long, a value outside a declared range
K.C. keyno two tuples agree on the primary keya second row arrives with a key that is already there
N.C. nullsa column declared NOT NULL has a valuea null is offered for such a column
E.I.C. entity integrityno part of a primary key is nulla null appears in any key column — which needs no declaration, it is automatic
R.I.C. referential integritya foreign key value exists in the referenced relation, or is nulla child points at a parent that is not there yet
The method, and the trap

Take the inserts strictly in order. “Assume the database is empty” is doing work: a row that would be fine later may fail now because the row it references has not been inserted yet. Most parts of this question turn on exactly that.

For each row, check in this order: are the values of the right type (D.C.); is any key column null (E.I.C.); is any NOT NULL column null (N.C.); is the key already present (K.C.); does every non-null foreign key find its parent (R.I.C.)?

A null foreign key is legal unless the column is part of the primary key or separately declared NOT NULL. “We do not know which vet” is a thing the relational model can say. This is the trap the question sets most often: students see a null in a foreign key column and call R.I.C., and R.I.C. is not violated — referential integrity is satisfied by a null.

Answer in two parts. Which statement fails, and which constraint. “The second insert fails, K.C.” is a complete answer; “fails” is not.

In each part the database is empty to begin with. Say whether the operation succeeds; if it does not, say which statement fails and which constraint it violates.

(a) Insert into clinic:

'CL09', 'Elmhurst Pet Care',       'Queens', '718-555-0109'
'CL09', 'Astoria Animal Hospital', 'Queens', '718-555-0110'

(b) Is it possible for treatment to hold these two rows at the same time?

'V014', 'x-ray', 140.00, 'imaging'
'V014', 'x-ray', 155.00, 'diagnostic'

(c) Insert into vet:

'VT09', 'CL99', 'Ruth Levine', 'general', 6

(d) Insert into clinic, then vet, then visit:

'CL05', 'Corona Animal Clinic', 'Queens', '718-555-0105'
'VT09', 'CL05', 'Ruth Levine', 'general', 6
'V900', 'PT07', 'VT09', '2026-09-01', 70.00

(a) Fails on the second row — K.C. clid is the primary key of clinic and 'CL09' is already there. The first insert succeeds; only the second is refused, which is why the question says which statement fails.

ERROR 1062 (23000): Duplicate entry 'CL09' for key 'PRIMARY'

Notice that the two rows disagree on everything except the key. That is not a defence: the key constraint is about the key alone.

(b) No — K.C. treatment is keyed by the pair (visid, tname), and both rows offer ('V014', 'x-ray'). The differing cost and category are irrelevant for the same reason as in (a).

ERROR 1062 (23000): Duplicate entry 'V014-x-ray' for key 'PRIMARY'

Change one of them and it is fine. ('V014', 'x-ray', ...) and ('V009', 'x-ray', ...) coexist happily — and in fact the database already holds an x-ray on three different visits. A composite key constrains the combination, not either column.

(c) Fails — R.I.C. clid in vet is a foreign key into clinic, and there is no clinic 'CL99' — the database is empty.

ERROR 1452 (23000): Cannot add or update a child row: a foreign key
constraint fails (`vetclinic`.`vet`, CONSTRAINT `vet_ibfk_1`
FOREIGN KEY (`clid`) REFERENCES `clinic` (`clid`))

Had the row been 'VT09', NULL, 'Ruth Levine', ... it would have succeeded. A null foreign key violates nothing: clid is not part of vet's primary key, so entity integrity does not apply, and referential integrity explicitly permits a null. It would mean “this vet is not attached to a clinic”, which the schema allows.

(d) Fails on the third statement — R.I.C. The clinic goes in; the vet goes in, because 'CL05' now exists; the visit is refused because pid is a foreign key into pet and there is no pet 'PT07'.

This is the shape the exam uses most: a chain of inserts where the first two are fine and the third points at something nobody created. Work down the sequence and check each foreign key against what exists at that moment, not against the full database you can see printed on the last page.

The fix is not to reorder these three — no order helps, because the pet is simply absent. Insert an owner and then a pet first, and the visit goes in.

M.7Problems 5 and 6 — algebra queries [9 + 15 pts]

Problem 5 is one query in relational algebra. Problem 6 is one query written twice, in algebra and then in SQL. Between them they are 24 points, and they are drawn from two patterns that come up again and again.

Pattern 1 — the second copy

Any question of the form “others who also ...” or “two different Xs that share a Y” needs the same relation twice. In the algebra that is numbered copies; in SQL it is two aliases. The giveaway in the English is the word other or also, and the condition that makes the two copies different is never optional:

σ[ ... ∧ name₄ ≠ 'Grace Abbott' ]

Leave it off and the answer includes the person you were comparing against, which the question explicitly excluded.

Pattern 2 — losers and winners

Any question containing never, no or not any needs a difference. A condition on one row can only say “this row is not X”; it can never say “this thing has no row that is X”. Those are different claims and the second is what is being asked.

So build the set of things that do offend, and subtract:

Losers  ←  Π[key] σ[the offending condition] ( ... )
Winners ←  Π[key] (everything)  −  Losers

Both operands must be projected onto the same attributes or the difference is undefined — which is the same rule as Problem 3, in use.

In SQL the difference becomes NOT IN against a subquery. In the algebra it is −. They are the same idea.

Most Problem 6 questions are this pattern and a positive condition: “never done A, and has at least one B”. Build the two sets separately and intersect, or build the B set and subtract the A set from it.

Find the names of all owners, other than 'Grace Abbott' herself, who have a pet that was seen by a vet who has also seen a pet of 'Grace Abbott'. Write the expression in relational algebra. 
HerVets ←  Π[vid₃] σ[F₁] ( owner₁ × pet₂ × visit₃ )

   F₁ ≡ [ name₁ = 'Grace Abbott' ∧ oid₁ = oid₂ ∧ pid₂ = pid₃ ]

Result  ←  Π[name₄] σ[F₂] ( HerVets₁ × visit₂ × pet₃ × owner₄ )

   F₂ ≡ [ vid₁ = vid₂ ∧ pid₂ = pid₃ ∧ oid₃ = oid₄
            ∧ name₄ ≠ 'Grace Abbott' ]

Running the SQL that corresponds to it:

+-------------+
| name        |
+-------------+
| Fiona Walsh |
| Hector Ruiz |
+-------------+

Name the intermediate relation. Written as one expression this needs six copies and a condition nobody can read. Two steps with a name in between is the same answer and a far better one to mark — and the exam paper tells you to show your work.

The vets who saw one of her pets are VT01, VT02 and VT03. Hector Ruiz's dog Pepper was seen by VT03; Fiona Walsh's dog Rufus was seen by VT01 and VT02. Mei Ling Tan's pets have only ever seen VT05, so she is correctly absent.

The last conjunct is the word “other”. Without it Grace Abbott appears in her own answer, since her pets were certainly seen by vets who saw her pets.

Comparing on the name, not the id. That is what the question gives you, and it is what the exam does. It is also a small lie: two owners could share a name. If you want to be exact, oid₄ ≠ oid₁ is the honest condition — and saying so in a margin note costs nothing and shows you noticed.

Find the ids and names of all owners who have never had a pet seen at a clinic in 'Brooklyn', and who have at least one visit with a fee above $200. (a) Write the expression in relational algebra. (b) Write the expression in SQL.

(a) Two sets, then a difference:

Brooklyn ←  Π[oid₁] σ[F₁] ( owner₁ × pet₂ × visit₃ × vet₄ × clinic₅ )

   F₁ ≡ [ oid₁ = oid₂ ∧ pid₂ = pid₃ ∧ vid₃ = vid₄
            ∧ clid₄ = clid₅ ∧ borough₅ = 'Brooklyn' ]

Spenders ←  Π[oid₁] σ[F₂] ( owner₁ × pet₂ × visit₃ )

   F₂ ≡ [ oid₁ = oid₂ ∧ pid₂ = pid₃ ∧ fee₃ > 200 ]

Winners  ←  Spenders − Brooklyn

Result   ←  Π[oid₂, name₂] σ[oid₁ = oid₂] ( Winners₁ × owner₂ )

The difference comes last but the projection comes first. Both operands are Π[oid], which is what makes the difference legal. Then one more product to turn the surviving ids back into names, because the question asked for both.

(b) In SQL the difference becomes NOT IN:

SELECT DISTINCT o.oid, o.name
  FROM owner o, pet p, visit v
 WHERE o.oid = p.oid AND p.pid = v.pid AND v.fee > 200
   AND o.oid NOT IN (SELECT o2.oid
                       FROM owner o2, pet p2, visit v2, vet vt2, clinic c2
                      WHERE o2.oid = p2.oid AND p2.pid = v2.pid
                        AND v2.vid = vt2.vid AND vt2.clid = c2.clid
                        AND c2.borough = 'Brooklyn');
+------+--------------+
| oid  | name         |
+------+--------------+
| OW03 | Mei Ling Tan |
| OW05 | Fiona Walsh  |
+------+--------------+

The subquery is the Brooklyn relation, written inline instead of named. The outer query is Spenders, and NOT IN is the minus sign. Line the two answers up against each other and every piece of the algebra is visible in the SQL.

Who it separates. Three owners have a visit over $200: Hector Ruiz (240), Mei Ling Tan (310) and Fiona Walsh (220). Hector's dog was seen at Harbor Vet Hospital, which is in Brooklyn, so he drops out. The other two stay.

DISTINCT is needed. An owner with two expensive visits would otherwise appear twice — the outer query is over visits, and the question is about owners.

The negative cannot be written as a condition. c.borough != 'Brooklyn' finds owners with some visit outside Brooklyn, which is a different set and includes Hector Ruiz, whose dog has also been seen in Queens. If you take one thing from this page, take that.

M.8Problems 6b, 7 and 8 — SQL queries [36 pts between them]

Three SQL answers, and they are three different questions. Problem 6(b) is the translation you have just seen. Problem 7 counts. Problem 8 groups. Knowing which of the three you are looking at is most of the work, because each has a shape that is almost always right.

Reading the question into a query

“How many distinct ...” → one row out, so no GROUP BY. SELECT COUNT(DISTINCT x), and the DISTINCT is not decoration: once you put four relations in a FROM clause, one vet with three visits is three rows.

“For each ...” → one row per something, so GROUP BY that something. Everything in the SELECT list must then be either grouped on or wrapped in an aggregate — that is the rule, and it is how you check your own answer.

“Ignore those with fewer than n ...” → HAVING, because it is a test on a whole group. “Ignore those whose rating is below ...” → WHERE, because it is a test on one row. Both say “ignore”; they go in different clauses, and Problem 8 nearly always asks for one of each.

“during 2026”, “in the fall of ...” → a range on a date column. BETWEEN '2026-01-01' AND '2026-12-31' is readable and inclusive at both ends. Do not reach for a function you are not sure of under exam pressure — two comparisons always work.

“from high to low” → ORDER BY ... DESC, and if you are ordering by an aggregate, write the aggregate again in the ORDER BY.

Find the number of distinct vets who saw a pet belonging to an owner in the zip code '11367' during 2026. Write the expression in SQL. 
SELECT COUNT(DISTINCT v.vid)
  FROM owner o, pet p, visit v
 WHERE o.oid = p.oid AND p.pid = v.pid
   AND o.zip = '11367'
   AND v.visitDate BETWEEN '2026-01-01' AND '2026-12-31';
+-----------------------+
| COUNT(DISTINCT v.vid) |
+-----------------------+
|                     3 |
+-----------------------+

Three relations, two links, two conditions. The chain is forced by the question: it names owners (by zip) and vets, and nothing joins those two except a visit, which reaches owners only through a pet.

Why DISTINCT is the whole question. Nine rows survive the WHERE clause — three owners live in 11367 and their pets made nine visits in 2026 between them. But those nine visits were handled by only three different vets, VT01, VT02 and VT05. Without DISTINCT the answer is 9, and 9 is not the number of vets.

No GROUP BY. The question asks for one number, not a number per anything. An aggregate with no GROUP BY collapses the whole result to a single row, which is exactly what is wanted.

The date range. Pepper's visit of 8 November 2025 and Mango's of 19 December 2025 are both excluded, correctly. Had the question said “the autumn of 2026”, only the two ends of the BETWEEN would change.

For each clinic in 'Queens', print the clinic ID, name, and the average fee of its visits. Count only visits handled by a vet with at least 5 years of experience, and ignore clinics with fewer than 4 such visits. Order the result by average fee, highest first. Write the expression in SQL. 
SELECT c.clid, c.name, AVG(v.fee)
  FROM clinic c, vet vt, visit v
 WHERE c.clid = vt.clid AND vt.vid = v.vid
   AND c.borough = 'Queens' AND vt.yearsExp >= 5
 GROUP BY c.clid, c.name
HAVING COUNT(v.visid) >= 4
 ORDER BY AVG(v.fee) DESC;
+------+---------------------+------------+
| clid | name                | AVG(v.fee) |
+------+---------------------+------------+
| CL03 | Parkview Pet Clinic | 109.000000 |
+------+---------------------+------------+

Two “ignores”, two different clauses. “Only visits by a vet with 5+ years” tests a single row, so it is in the WHERE and happens before grouping. “Clinics with fewer than 4 such visits” tests a whole group, so it is in the HAVING and happens after. Swap them and the query is either rejected or quietly wrong.

Taking the filters off one at a time shows each one working. Without the HAVING:

+------+-----------------------+--------+------------+
| clid | name                  | visits | avgfee     |
+------+-----------------------+--------+------------+
| CL01 | Riverside Animal Care |      3 | 150.000000 |
| CL03 | Parkview Pet Clinic   |      5 | 109.000000 |
+------+-----------------------+--------+------------+

and without the experience condition as well:

+------+-----------------------+--------+------------+
| clid | name                  | visits | avgfee     |
+------+-----------------------+--------+------------+
| CL01 | Riverside Animal Care |      6 | 111.666667 |
| CL03 | Parkview Pet Clinic   |      5 | 109.000000 |
+------+-----------------------+--------+------------+

Riverside's average moves from 111.67 to 150.00 when the junior vet's three cheap visits come out, and then the clinic disappears entirely because three is fewer than four. Both filters change the answer, which is how you know you put them in the right places.

Bayside Animal Centre never appears at all, in any of the three. It is in Queens, but it has no vets, so no row of the product mentions it and no group is formed for it. GROUP BY groups the rows it is given; it cannot invent a group with nothing in it. If the question had wanted Bayside listed with a zero, that would need an outer join — which is the one thing this course's SQL deliberately cannot do, and worth saying in a margin note if you think a grader might wonder.

c.name is in the GROUP BY even though c.clid already determines it. Standard SQL insists, and this is the kind of thing a marker checks.

The ORDER BY repeats the aggregate. You may instead write AVG(v.fee) AS avgfee in the SELECT list and order by avgfee, which is shorter and reads better.

If you have one evening

Work §M.4's Practice A with the answers closed. Then Practice D, which is the single most representative question on this page — a negative, a positive, algebra and SQL in one. Then re-draw two diagrams from Set 1 and map one of them with Set 3. That is about two hours and it touches 70 of the 100 points.

In the room

Tear off the last page first. Then read the schema once before reading any question: where the keys are and which way the foreign keys point is the information you will use five times.

Do Problem 3 early. Twenty points, no narrative to interpret, and the impossible parts are nearly free once you have checked the arity.

45 seconds a point. That is about six minutes on Problem 1 and nine on Problem 3. If a diagram is eating fifteen minutes, leave it, take the query marks, and come back.

Write the condition out. On every algebra answer, name the intermediate relations and put F ≡ [ ... ] on its own line. It is faster to write than a nested one-liner, far easier to correct, and it is what “show all work” means here.

Before you hand in, check three things: every edge of Problem 1 has a cardinality at both ends; every schema in Problem 2 has its foreign keys listed as well as its primary key; and every SELECT list is either grouped on or aggregated.

What is not on it

Everything up to Lecture 12 is examinable: ER and EER modelling, the mapping to relations, the relational model and its constraints, relational algebra, and SQL. Normalization, functional dependencies, transactions, concurrency, recovery and indexing are not — they come after the midterm. If you find yourself revising 3NF for this exam, you are revising for the next one.
