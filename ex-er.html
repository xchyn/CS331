Exercise set 1 · after the ER-modeling lectures ER modeling

Drawing the diagram

Nineteen exercises, sixteen of which ask for a complete ER diagram, ending with two large ones. What deserves to be an entity set and what is only a value; keys, weak entity sets and partial keys; cardinality ratios, participation constraints, and where an attribute belongs when it describes a pair rather than a thing.

An ER diagram is not a picture of a database. It is an argument about the part of the world the database is supposed to describe, written in a notation compact enough that somebody can disagree with it. Almost every mistake in this set is the same mistake in different clothes — a design decision made silently, so that nobody, including the designer, can see what was decided.

So the discipline this set drills is not draughtsmanship. It is saying out loud, for every box and every line you draw, what fact about the world made you draw it that way. The problems come from the CSCI 331 problem book; the solutions show the route to the diagram, not just the diagram.

Plain ER only

This set stays inside the basic entity-relationship model: entity sets, relationship sets, attributes, cardinality and participation. Subclasses, specialization and the d/o constraints belong to the enhanced model, EER, and have a set of their own — Set 2. If a problem here tempts you to draw a subclass, model it with an attribute or a separate entity set instead — that is the exercise.

1.1Entities, values and attributes

An entity is a thing with an identity of its own — it stays the same thing even when everything recorded about it changes. A value has no identity: it is entirely described by what it is. A patient whose name, address and diagnosis all change is still that patient; a temperature of 38.4° is nothing but 38.4°, and two of them are the same thing. That is the whole test, and it settles most of the "should this be a box?" arguments before they start.

An entity set's attributes come in four flavours that the notation distinguishes, because each one is a different promise to whoever implements the schema:

kindwhat it meansexample
simpleone atomic value per entitya person's name
compositea structure with named componentsaddress = street, city, state, zip
multivalueda set of values, possibly emptya person's home phone #
derivednot stored; computed from other valuesage, from birth date and today
Why derived attributes are drawn at all

If a derived attribute is never stored, why put it in the diagram? Because the diagram is a specification, and "the database must be able to answer this" is part of the specification. Drawing age dashed, with a line to birth date, records both that users will ask for age and that storing it would be a bug: it changes every day, and a stored copy goes stale the moment nobody updates it.

A third question decides more diagrams than either of those: what is an attribute an attribute of? If its value is determined by a combination of entities rather than by any one of them, it is an attribute of the relationship and it hangs off the diamond. A price charged by a particular supplier for a particular medication is the standard case, and it turns up in five of the exercises below.

1.2Keys, weak entity sets, partial keys

An attribute K is a key of an entity set S if no two distinct entities in S have equal values of K. Note what this says: it is a constraint on every possible state of the database, not an observation about the rows that happen to be there today. Two students in your section having different birthdays does not make birthday a key.

Some entity sets have no key of their own. A weak entity set is identified only through its relationship to an owner: its partial key distinguishes it among the entities that share one owner, and nothing more. The identifying relationship is drawn as a double diamond, and participation on the weak side is always total — a weak entity that is not owned by anything cannot be identified, so it cannot exist.

The classic slip

"Seat 14 appears twice in the table, so seat # is not a key — the design is broken." It is not broken. Seat 14 of Kiely 170 and seat 14 of Science Building 137 are different seats, and the partial key is doing exactly its job: it is unique within one classroom. What identifies a seat is the pair (room #, seat #), and that pair is assembled by the relationship, not stored in the weak entity set.

1.3Cardinality and participation

Every binary relationship carries two independent constraints, and students lose marks by reading one and answering the other.

  • The cardinality ratio — 1:1, 1:M or M:N — says how many entities on one side may be related to one entity on the other.
  • The participation constraint says whether an entity must take part at all: total (double line) or partial (single line).

They are orthogonal. "Each campus belongs to exactly one university" is two statements: at most one (the ratio) and at least one (total participation). A diagram that shows only the first has not said that a campus cannot float free.

A relationship may also join an entity set to itself. Then the diagram would be ambiguous without role names, because the two lines go to the same box and nothing else distinguishes them.

A relationship has attributes of its own — say a price on the relationship between suppliers and medications. Could you move price onto the medication entity set instead?

Only if every supplier charges the same price for a given medication, which is exactly what the scenario denies. A price belongs to a (supplier, medication) pair, so it belongs to the relationship.

One case is different. On a 1:M relationship an attribute of the relationship can always be moved to the entity set on the M side, because each entity there takes part at most once. That is a legitimate simplification — but it is a decision worth making deliberately, not by accident.

1.4The notation on one page

Every symbol used on this page and in the exercises below. Nothing here is decorative — each shape states a constraint, and leaving one out is asserting the default, not staying silent.

Entity set
a thing with its own identity

Weak entity set
identified only through an owner

Relationship set
an association among entities

Identifying relationship
supplies a weak set its identity

Attribute
one atomic value per entity

Key attribute
underlined; unique across the set

Partial key
dashed; unique within one owner

Multivalued
a set of values, possibly empty

Derived
computed, never stored

A double line from an entity set to a relationship is total participation; a single line is partial. The cardinality ratio goes on the line as 1, M or N. Every relationship line on this page is plain: the ratio is written out at each end rather than shown with an arrowhead, so a line never has to be read twice.

1.5Exercises

Three short questions to warm up, then sixteen diagrams. For every diagram, indicate carefully all the applicable constraints — a diagram without its cardinality ratios and participation lines is not finished, however many boxes it has. Draw before you click.

Three to warm up

You are designing a database for a travel bureau. Will you model air temperature as a value or as an entity? Explain your decision.

A value. Air temperature has no identity of its own; it is adequately described by a number in a certain range, and the simple arithmetic operations already available for numbers — comparison, addition, subtraction — cover the expected usage of the temperature type.

The method. Ask two questions of any candidate. First, does it have an identity that survives a change in its description? A patient renamed is the same patient; a temperature changed from 31° to 32° is simply a different temperature. Second, does anything need to be said about it that is not part of what it is? Nothing hangs off 31°. Both answers point the same way, so it is a value.

Change the enterprise and the answer can change with it. For a climate research lab a reading — taken at a place, at a time, by an instrument, flagged as suspect or not — is very much an entity. The temperature itself is still just one of its attributes.

Give a precise description of a situation where two distinct entities of the same type have identical values of the key attribute. Provide a reasonable example. If such a situation is impossible, explain why.

It is impossible, by definition: two distinct entities of the same type cannot agree on the value of the key.

The method. When a question asks you to produce something impossible, the marks are for recognising that the definition already forbids it, and for saying which definition. Do not search for a clever counter-example; there is none, and the search is the trap. The related situation that is possible — and which the question is quietly testing — is two entities of different types sharing a key value, which is no contradiction at all, since each key constrains only its own entity set.

Give a precise description of a situation where two distinct entities of the same type have identical values of the partial key attribute. Provide a reasonable example. If such a situation is impossible, explain why.

It is possible. The partial key value is unique only among those entities of a weak entity type that are related to the same entity of the strong (owner, governing) entity type. Hence two distinct entities that have different owners can agree on the value of the partial key.

Example. Two entities of the weak type student seat can be assigned the same seat number, if they are not in the same classroom. The diagram is the one in §1.2 above.

The method. Compare the two definitions word by word. Uniqueness of a key is over the whole entity set; uniqueness of a partial key is over one owner's entities. The pair (owner's key, partial key) is unique across the whole weak set — which is precisely why the weak set needs the identifying relationship in order to be identified at all.

One relationship at a time

Give a reasonable example of a recursive relationship in the entity set persons. Draw the ER diagram, indicating carefully the cardinality and participation constraints.

The example. The relationship "is mother" is functional (many-to-one) from children to mothers, since everybody has a unique mother, but a mother may have many children. The participation is total on the side of the children and partial on the side of the mothers.

The method. Three decisions, in this order.

  1. One box or two? Mothers are persons and children are persons, so one box. Drawing mother and child as separate entity sets would claim they are different kinds of thing, and then a woman who is both would have to appear twice.
  2. Role names. With both lines running to the same box, the diagram is ambiguous without them. They go on the lines, not in the diamond.
  3. The constraints, one side at a time. Reading the child line: each person has exactly one mother — ratio 1 on the mother side, and total participation on the child side. Reading the mother line: a person may have any number of children including none — ratio M, partial participation.

Total participation on the child side is the interesting claim: it says the database may not hold a person whose mother is unknown. For an enterprise that carries it through, fine — but notice it is an assertion about the world, and the kind that makes a system reject a real person at three in the morning. Exercise 1.7 is the same relationship with the opposite decision, and the wording is what forces the difference.

Give a reasonable example of a relationship with cardinality ratio 1 : M between any two entity sets of your choice. Draw the ER diagram, indicating carefully the cardinality and participation constraints.

The example. The relationship between campuses and the universities they belong to is functional (many-to-one) from campuses to universities, since every campus belongs to a university, but a university may operate many campuses — CUNY, for instance. The participation is total on both sides, since every campus must be owned, and every university must have at least one place to operate on.

The method. Write the two sentences before you draw anything. "A campus belongs to at most one university" fixes the ratio. "A campus belongs to at least one university" fixes the participation on the campus side. Then repeat in the other direction: "a university has at least one campus" gives total participation there too, while "a university may have many campuses" is what makes the ratio 1 : M rather than 1 : 1.

Each of the four claims is separately arguable, which is the point of drawing them. A university between campuses — newly chartered, or having closed its last site — would break the total participation on the university side, and if the enterprise wants to record such a university, that line must become single.

Give a reasonable example of a relationship with cardinality ratio M : M between any two entity sets of your choice. Draw the ER diagram, indicating carefully the cardinality and participation constraints.

The example. The relationship between professors and the subjects they teach is many-to-many, since a professor may teach many subjects and a subject may be taught by many professors. Realistically, participation should be total on both sides. However, we may need to deal with subjects that were never taught, and professors that never lectured — which suggests optional participation on both sides.

The method. The ratio is the easy half: find one entity on each side that is related to two on the other, and M : N is forced. The participation is the half worth thinking about, and the answer above shows the thinking rather than hiding it: the ideal claim (everyone teaches, everything is taught) collides with the cases the database must nonetheless hold — the new appointment who has not yet been given a section, the subject in the catalogue that has not run for years. Partial participation is chosen to accommodate them.

The general rule: total participation is a promise that the system will refuse an entity that has no partner. Make it only where you are willing for that refusal to happen.

The hospital database

Each of the next three describes part of one hospital database. Draw carefully the complete ER diagram for that part, indicating all the applicable constraints.

Each patient is identified by social security number. The hospital needs to know the name and age of the patient, as well as the personal information of the patient's mother, if she has ever been a patient at the hospital.

Step 1 — how many entity sets? One. The mother's "personal information" is the same information a patient has, and the hospital only wants it if she has ever been a patient — so she is already in patient. A separate mother box would duplicate every woman who is both.

Step 2 — attributes and the key. id # is underlined: the problem says patients are identified by it. name is simple.

Step 3 — age. The hospital needs to know the age, but age is derived — it changes every birthday. Store birth date and draw age dashed, joined to the entity set like any other attribute. Writing age as a stored attribute is the single most common error on this question.

Step 4 — the relationship and its constraints. "Is mother" is recursive, with role names mother and child. The ratio is 1 : M: one mother, many children. Participation is partial on both sides — note the single lines, and note that this is the opposite of Exercise 1.4. The wording forces it: "if she has ever been a patient" concedes that most patients' mothers are not on file, so the database must accept a patient with no mother recorded.

The hospital pharmacy buys medications from suppliers, whose names, addresses and contact phone numbers are kept on file. Each medication is identified by a generic name. A particular medication may be offered by several suppliers, though not necessarily at the same price. Additionally, suppliers are free to assign their own name (so called brand name) to each medication they sell. The pharmacy keeps track of the brand names under which various suppliers offer the medications, as well as of the prices they charge.

Step 1 — two entity sets. supplier and medication, with gen. name underlined as the key of the latter, as the problem states.

Step 2 — the plural in "phone numbers". Contact phone numbers, plural, with nothing said about how many: a multivalued attribute, drawn as a double ellipse. This is the whole of the phrase's content, and it is easy to read past.

Step 3 — the ratio. A medication may be offered by several suppliers, and a supplier plainly sells several medications: M : N.

Step 4 — and now the real question. Where do price and brand name go? Not on medication: the same medication has a different price from each supplier, so a single price attribute could not hold them. Not on supplier: a supplier has one price per medication, not one price. Each value is determined by the pair, so both are attributes of the relationship offer, and they hang off the diamond.

That is the general test, and it is worth memorising: an attribute belongs to the relationship exactly when its value depends on the combination of entities rather than on any one of them. Every M : N relationship in a real schema should prompt the question.

The hospital operates a number of off-site out-patient facilities, each identified by a short name. The administration needs to know the address of each facility and their emergency contact phone numbers. The hospital also has a number of ambulance cars, dedicated to the transport of out-patients. For each car, the hospital records its license plate number and the date its registration expires. Every car is managed by one out-patient facility, to which it is assigned on a long-term basis. The date of the assignment is kept on file.

Step 1 — the keys are given. "Each identified by a short name" underlines name on facility; a car is identified by its license #. Take keys from the wording whenever the wording supplies them.

Step 2 — "phone numbers", again plural. Multivalued, double ellipse. The same phrase as in Exercise 1.8, doing the same job.

Step 3 — the two constraints on assigned, separately. "Every car is managed by one facility" is the cardinality ratio: 1 : M, one facility to many cars. "Every car is managed" is participation: total on the car side, drawn as a double line. Two different words in one sentence, two different marks. Nothing says a facility must have a car, so its participation stays partial.

Step 4 — the assignment date. The date belongs to the assignment, not to the car and not to the facility, so it hangs off the diamond. Because the ratio is 1 : M it could legitimately be moved onto car instead — each car is assigned at most once, so there is room for exactly one date there. Leaving it on the relationship is the more honest diagram: it says the date is a fact about the assignment, which is what it is.

Rotund Trend

Rotund Trend is a respectable pizza restaurant chain with several outlets in the town. The next three exercises each model one part of its database.

Each restaurant location is identified by name, which is always an interesting phrase, carefully chosen to capture customer attention. In addition to the name, the company archives the address of each restaurant, and the addresses of all (if any) convenient parking garages, recommended to walk-in customers.

Step 1 — one entity set, and the key. outlet, with name underlined. The business about interesting phrases is flavour; "identified by name" is the content.

Step 2 — the phrase that carries the whole exercise. "the addresses of all (if any) convenient parking garages". All makes it a set; if any makes the set possibly empty. That is precisely the definition of a multivalued attribute, so parking is a double ellipse.

Step 3 — what you should not do. Garages have no attributes here beyond their address and nothing is ever said about them, so promoting parking to its own entity set with a relationship buys nothing and asserts more than the problem says — in particular it would let two outlets share one garage entity, a claim the text never makes. Model what you were told, and no more.

Rotund Trend owns a number of delivery cars. Cars are identified by license plate number, and each car is characterized by manufacturer's name, production year, and delivery capacity. Each car is serviced by a number of dealers, chosen according to their perceived expertise. An individual dealer may receive service contracts for several cars from the delivery fleet. However, a contact person is designated by the dealer for each individual car with the service contract, to guarantee that Rotund Trend has a reliable source of information about the history of each of its cars. The database records names and addresses of all dealers, one phone number per dealer, as well as the name of contact persons for individual cars.

Step 1 — the two entity sets and their keys. car, keyed by license plate # as stated, with manufacturer, year and capacity. dealer, keyed by name, with address and one phone # — "one phone number per dealer" is there specifically to tell you this one is not multivalued, in contrast with the hospital problems.

Step 2 — the ratio. "Each car is serviced by a number of dealers" and "an individual dealer may receive contracts for several cars" are the two halves of M : N, stated one after the other. Participation is total on the car side — every car in the fleet is serviced — and partial on the dealer side, since nothing says a dealer on file must currently hold a contract.

Step 3 — where the contact person goes. This is the exercise. The contact person is designated "by the dealer for each individual car": change either the dealer or the car and you get a different contact person. So it is determined by the pair, and it is an attribute of service contract. Putting it on dealer would allow one contact person per dealer, which contradicts the text; putting it on car would allow one per car, which contradicts it in the other direction.

Rotund Trend stores information about its customers. Respecting fully the privacy of its customers, Rotund Trend identifies them by internal customer code, assigned to them upon their first walk-in visit. The company needs to know the (preferred) name and address of each customer. The company practices customer sponsorship — each customer (if they so desire) is welcome to designate one of other customers as their sponsor, with the understanding that the sponsor deserves credit for recommending Rotund Trend to the sponsored customer. The management records the amount of discount credit that has been given away to all individual instances of sponsorship.

Step 1 — one entity set. Sponsors are customers, so sponsor is a recursive relationship on customer, exactly as "is mother" was recursive on persons. code is the key.

Step 2 — the role names. sponsoring and sponsored. Without them the diagram cannot say which end is which, and the 1 and the M become meaningless.

Step 3 — the ratio, from one phrase. "designate one of other customers as their sponsor" — at most one sponsor per customer, so 1 on the sponsoring side, M on the sponsored side. A customer may sponsor many.

Step 4 — participation. Partial on both sides, and the parenthesis "(if they so desire)" is what tells you. Sponsorship is optional; a customer with no sponsor must be storable, and so must a customer who has never sponsored anyone.

Step 5 — the credit. "given away to all individual instances of sponsorship" — an instance of sponsorship is a relationship instance, so credit given is an attribute of sponsor. The problem could hardly say so more explicitly, and it is still the step most often missed.

The course schedule

Dangerous Professor is making the course schedule for the entire Department of Computer Science, as her service duty. Instructors are identified by social security number and courses by course number.

Based on the instructors' recommendations and those of the Chair (who urges all instructors to be prepared to teach as many different courses as possible), the Professor records the eligibility of individual instructors to teach specific courses. She identifies each course by number, and writes down its name and number of credit hours. Whenever an instructor is able to teach a certain course, the Professor makes a note of it, recording also the number of sections of that course that the instructor has ever taught for the Department in the past, as well as the year when they taught one such section for the first time. Additionally, every instructor is welcome to name one classroom as their favorite, and the Professor is willing to bear their preference in mind. Each classroom has a College-wide unique room number, and the Professor keeps track of the capacity of each classroom.

Step 1 — three entity sets, three keys. instructor keyed by socSecNum, course by number, classroom by roomNumber. Every one of the three keys is stated in the text; none of them is your invention.

Step 2 — eligibility is a relationship, not an attribute. "Whether instructor i can teach course c" is a fact about a pair, and many instructors relate to many courses: M : N, drawn as can teach. Participation is partial on both sides — a new instructor eligible for nothing yet, a course nobody is eligible for.

Step 3 — and its attributes. "the number of sections of that course that the instructor has ever taught" names both entities in one breath, so # sections sits on the relationship. So does year, for the same reason: it is the year that instructor first taught that course. Hanging either one on course or on instructor would lose the pairing and give a wrong answer to an obvious query.

Step 4 — the favourite classroom. "one classroom" fixes the ratio at M : 1 from instructor to classroom, so the M sits on the instructor end of likes and the 1 on the classroom end. "Every instructor is welcome to name one" makes participation partial: an instructor with no favourite is perfectly legal.

The Professor assigns one instructor to each course section. Typically, more than one section of a course is offered. To distinguish between different sections of a single course, the Professor labels them as "A", "B", "C", etc., and settles the meeting time for each section, as well as the maximum number of students that are entitled to enroll.

Step 1 — why section is weak. The labels are "A", "B", "C" — and every course has a section A. "Section A" identifies nothing on its own; only "section A of CSCI 331" does. So section is a weak entity set, letter is its partial key (dashed underline), and has is the identifying relationship, drawn as a double diamond. This is the single most important judgement in the exercise, and the phrase "to distinguish between different sections of a single course" is what settles it.

Step 2 — the constraints on has. One course has many sections, so 1 : M. Participation on the section side is total, drawn double — and it is not a judgement call: a weak entity that is not related to an owner cannot be identified, so the total line always comes with the double diamond.

Step 3 — the attributes of a section. time and max are settled per section, so they go on section.

Step 4 — assigning the instructor. "assigns one instructor to each section" is both constraints in five words: ratio 1 : M, and total participation on the section side, since every section gets one. An instructor with no sections this term is fine, so that side stays partial.

Two owners, one weak set

Notice that section is related to both course and instructor, but only has is an identifying relationship. Being related to something does not make it an owner; the owner is the entity set whose key you need in order to tell two weak entities apart. Swapping the instructor in week three does not change which section it is.

Midnight Candle

Midnight Candle is a chain of popular libraries with excellent reputation nation-wide, operating at least one branch office in almost every town in the country.

Branch offices are identified by name, and the information kept on file about each office contains the address of the office, one phone number, one Web URL, and the area (in square feet) of the available shelf space. The library owns many copies of each book, and these copies travel among branch offices, as the need arises. The library identifies books by ISBN number, and records the title of each book. Copies of the same book are distinguished by copy number, assigned internally. For each copy, the library keeps a numerical grade that describes visual appeal of that copy. The library needs to know the current location (branch office) of each copy of each book, as well as the day when the branch office received the copy.

Step 1 — three entity sets, and one of them is weak. "Copies of the same book are distinguished by copy number" — distinguished among copies of the same book, so copy 3 of one title and copy 3 of another are different copies with the same number. copy is weak, copy # is its partial key, and book (keyed by ISBN) is the owner through a double diamond.

Step 2 — branch offices. Keyed by name, with address, phone #, URL and area. Note "one phone number, one Web URL": simple attributes, stated as such, in deliberate contrast with the multivalued phone numbers elsewhere in the problem book. The text tells you the cardinality of every attribute if you read it for that.

Step 3 — location as a relationship. The current location is a branch office, and the office changes over time, so it is a relationship in, not an attribute. "the current location of each copy" is one office per copy: ratio M : 1 from copy to branch, and participation total on the copy side — every copy is physically somewhere.

Step 4 — the day received. It is the day that office received that copy, so date belongs to in. Since the ratio is M : 1 it could be moved onto copy without loss, as in Exercise 1.9 — and here, as there, the relationship is where it says what it means.

What this design cannot do

It records where each copy is now, and when it arrived there. It does not record where the copy has been: the moment a copy moves, the old (branch, date) pair is overwritten and the history is gone. If the library wanted the travel history, one relationship instance per stay would be needed — which in this notation means a weak entity set stay, owned by the copy and partially keyed by the arrival date. Worth noticing that the specification, as written, does not ask for it.

Branch offices of Midnight Candle sell various items, for convenience of its diligent readers (such as reading lamps, tables, chairs, book-marks, candy, etc.) The library keeps a detailed catalog of these items, identifying them by item number (assigned internally). Each item has a name, a short description of its purpose, and sometimes one or more photographs. The library purchases these items from various suppliers, whose company names and addresses are stored. For each purchase of each item, a purchase order is generated with a unique order number, listing the supplier, item, number of units bought, price per unit, and the due delivery date.

Step 1 — the purchase order is an entity set. It has a unique order number of its own, so it is identified without reference to anything else — a strong entity set, not a relationship and not a weak set. Students who model the purchase as an M : N relationship between item and supplier lose the ability to place two separate orders for the same item from the same supplier, which is an ordinary thing to want to do.

Step 2 — "sometimes one or more photographs". A set, possibly empty: photo is multivalued. Same phrase-reading as the parking garages in Exercise 1.10.

Step 3 — two relationships, both functional. One order lists one supplier and one item, so for and from are each M : 1 out of order — the M on each order end, the 1 on item and on supplier. Participation is total on the order side — an order naming no item or no supplier is not an order — and partial on the other two, since a catalogued item need never have been ordered.

Step 4 — where units, price and due date go. On order. Because both relationships are functional out of the order, each order has exactly one item and one supplier, so these values are facts about the order itself; there is nothing to be gained by hanging them on a diamond. Compare Exercise 1.8, where the price genuinely could not live on either entity set — the difference is that there was no order entity there to carry it.

Two more

The personnel office at a college maintains a faculty database. The office records the name, social security number, rank and salary of every professor. Each professor belongs to one department, considered as his main affiliation, but may belong to others as well. The office keeps track of all these affiliations. The office needs to know the names of all departments, and the room number and phone number of each department office. Every department has a single chair-person (who is also a professor.) Whereas a department chair, as a professor, may belong to several departments, he can be a chairman of only one of them.

Step 1 — two entity sets, two relationships between them. That is the shape of this problem, and it is the thing to recognise. member records affiliation; chair records who runs the department. Two different facts about the same pair of entity sets need two diamonds — merging them into one relationship with a "role" attribute would make the 1:1 constraint on chairing inexpressible.

Step 2 — member. A professor belongs to one department and "may belong to others as well", and a department plainly has many professors: M : N. Participation is total on both sides — every professor belongs to at least one department, and a department with no members is not a department.

Step 3 — chair. "a single chair-person" and "he can be a chairman of only one of them" give 1 : 1 in both directions. Participation is total on the department side (every department has a chair) and partial on the professor side (most professors chair nothing).

Step 4 — the question the problem book asks about this diagram. Which aspect of the specification is not implemented by this design? The main affiliation. The text says each professor belongs to one department "considered as his main affiliation", plus others; the diagram records the affiliations as an undifferentiated M : N set and loses which one is primary.

Two honest repairs. Add a boolean-valued attribute is main to member — cheap, but the constraint "exactly one per professor" cannot be drawn in this notation and would have to be written beside the diagram. Or add a third relationship main affiliation, 1 : M from department to professor and total on the professor side, which does state the constraint in the diagram at the cost of a redundancy: every main affiliation is also a membership, and nothing in the diagram forces the two to agree.

Either way, the discipline is the one this whole set is built around: when the notation cannot say something the specification requires, say so explicitly rather than letting the diagram quietly drop the requirement.

Two big ones

The last two are larger than anything above: one asks you to put two diagrams you have already drawn into a single picture, the other builds six entity sets from scratch. Give each of them a full sheet of paper and expect to redraw once. There is no trick in either — only more of the same decisions, and more chances to make one of them silently.

Draw a complete ER diagram for the whole course-schedule database — the eligibility of instructors to teach courses, their favourite classrooms, and the sections with their assigned instructors — that is, one diagram covering everything specified in Exercises 1.13 and 1.14 together.

The point of the exercise. Combining two correct diagrams is not copying them side by side. Four things have to be settled that neither half had to settle on its own.

1. One instructor box, one course box. Each appeared in both halves; each appears once here. Every relationship that touched either one now touches the same box, which is what makes the diagram a single schema rather than two.

2. instructor now carries three relationships — likes, can teach and assign — and they say different things. Eligibility (can teach) is a standing fact; assignment (assign) is this term's decision. A student who merges them loses the ability to record an instructor who is eligible for a course but not teaching it this term, which is most of the table.

3. section is weak with respect to course only. It is related to instructor as well, but that relationship is not identifying and is drawn with a single diamond. Changing who teaches section A in week three does not make it a different section; changing the course would. The owner is whichever entity set you need the key of in order to tell two sections apart, and here that is course.

4. Nothing new is invented. Every attribute, ratio and participation line is exactly the one the two halves already justified. If anything changed when you merged, one of the two halves was wrong — go back and find which.

On laying out a big diagram

Place the entity sets first, in a rough grid, with the ones that relate to each other near each other; add the relationship diamonds on the lines between them; hang the attributes off the outside edges last, pointing away from the centre. Crossing lines are allowed and sometimes unavoidable — but if your diagram has many, the boxes are usually in the wrong order, and moving one is quicker than redrawing thirty lines.

Draw a complete ER diagram for the bibliography database. Dangerous Professor keeps track of a variety of topics that occur in the subject matter of various courses taught by her. Topics are identified by unique names, in the Professor's own naming scheme. Each topic is classified as belonging to one or more subject areas — graph algorithms, for instance, is one area in which the topic of topological sorting appears. The Professor assigns an overall significance grade to each topic. An individual topic may appear in any number of books. The Professor needs to know all the books that address a given topic, and she assigns a number that estimates how well an individual book presents the given topic. Books are identified by ISBN, and the Professor stores the title, author, and year of publication of each book. The Professor teaches one or more sections of a certain number of courses in her department. Courses are identified by name (e.g. databases). Each section, at a given time, is identified by its 4-digit code (such as 0951 in the Spring of 2002). The Professor numbers the lectures in an individual course offering, and assigns one topic to each lecture, though this assignment varies from semester to semester, and even from section to section, when she teaches multiple sections of the same course.

Step 1 — find the entity sets. Six: subject, topic, textbook, course, section and lecture. The keys the text gives you are topic.name ("identified by unique names"), textbook.ISBN and course.name. Subject areas are named, so subject.name is the key.

Step 2 — the two M : N relationships. A topic belongs to "one or more" subject areas and a subject area holds many topics, so under is M : N, total on the topic side. A topic appears in any number of books and a book addresses many topics, so coverage is M : N too — and the number estimating "how well an individual book presents the given topic" is determined by the pair, so presentation hangs off that diamond. Same test as the pharmacy in Exercise 1.8.

Step 3 — two weak entity sets, nested. This is the spine of the diagram.

  • A section is identified by a 4-digit code "at a given time" — codes are reused across terms and courses, so section is weak, owned by course through offered, with 4digitCode as its partial key and year and semester as ordinary attributes.
  • Lectures are numbered within an offering — every section has a lecture 1 — so lecture is weak in turn, owned by section through contain, partially keyed by number.

A lecture is therefore identified by (course name, 4-digit code, number): a weak entity set whose owner is itself weak inherits the whole chain. Both identifying relationships get a double diamond and a double line on the weak side.

Step 4 — where the topic attaches, and why it matters. The obvious wrong answer is to relate topic to course: courses are about topics, after all. But read the last sentence again. The assignment "varies from semester to semester, and even from section to section" — so the thing that has one topic is not the course, and not even the section, but the individual lecture. syllabus is M : 1 from lecture to topic: one topic per lecture, a topic used in many lectures.

That single decision is what the whole problem is built to test, and it is the reason the lectures had to be modelled at all. Attach the topic to the course and the database can no longer answer "what did I actually cover in section 0951 last spring?", which is the question the Professor is keeping the database for.

A check worth running on any big diagram

For each entity set, ask what a single entity of it is, out loud, in one sentence: "a lecture is the n-th meeting of one section of one course". If the sentence needs the word and to hold two unrelated things together, the box is doing two jobs and should be split. If you cannot say the sentence without naming another entity set, the box is weak — and if you have not drawn a double diamond, you have a bug.

Going further

Every diagram on this page is a specification waiting to become tables. The mapping is mechanical — each entity set becomes a relation, each 1 : M relationship becomes a foreign key on the M side, each M : N relationship becomes a relation of its own carrying both keys plus the relationship's attributes, and a weak entity set takes its owner's key into its own primary key. Once you have seen that mapping, the constraints on this page stop being notation and start being the difference between a schema that can hold a wrong state and one that cannot.
