Exercise set 5 · Lectures 8 and 9 Relational algebra

A set of operations that apply to relations and produce relations

The five operations in the order the lectures give them, then twenty-two exercises: eight on evaluating an expression (or saying precisely why it has none), seven of the lecture's own queries against the College database, and seven more against the Professor Payroll database. No expression on this page uses the join — §5.2 says why.

The relational algebra is small enough to learn in an afternoon and strange enough to take a week. Two things make it strange. It has no quantifiers, so every no, never and not has to be rebuilt out of set difference. And it has no variables, so “two different departments” has to be rebuilt out of two copies of one relation. Sections 5.4 and 5.5 are those two tricks; everything else is bookkeeping.

5.1The five operations

A set of operations that apply to relations and produce a relation. Each one is defined by what its result's schema is and what its result's content is.

① Set operations — union, intersection and difference, for union compatible relations: the two schemas must be the same, including the domains.

Schema:   same as R, or S

Content:  t ∈ R ∪ S  ⇔  t ∈ R  or   t ∈ S
          t ∈ R ∩ S  ⇔  t ∈ R  and  t ∈ S
          t ∈ R − S  ⇔  t ∈ R  and  t ∉ S        (also written R \ S)

② Selection — σ[F] R selects the tuples of R that satisfy the filter formula F.

Schema:   R

Content:  t ∈ σ[F] R  ⇔  t ∈ R  and  F(t)

③ Projection — Π[D] R selects certain attributes D from R.

Schema:   D

Content:  t ∈ Π[D] R  ⇔  (∃ w ∈ R)( w[D] = t )

④ Product — R × S, the cartesian product of R[A1,…,An] and S[B1,…,Bm].

Schema:   A1, ..., An, B1, ..., Bm

Content:  t ∈ R × S  ⇔  (∃ v ∈ R)(∃ w ∈ S)
                            ( t[A1..An] = v  and  t[B1..Bm] = w )

          |R × S|  =  |R| · |S|

⑤ Join — R ⋈ S combines related tuples from two relations by matching on every attribute they share. It is defined for completeness; no expression on this page uses it, and §5.2 says why. What matters here is the identity the lectures end on:

R ⋈ S  =  Π[the shared attributes once, then the rest] σ[F] ( R × S )
    where   F  ≡  [ every attribute R and S share is equal on both sides ]

— a join is a product, a selection and a projection, written short.

Notation on this page

A subscript is written in square brackets immediately after its operator, so that an expression fits on one line of monospace: Π[tid] σ[F] Teacher. A long filter formula is given a name — F, G — and written underneath, exactly as in the lectures. Copies of a relation in one product are numbered by appending a digit, so Teacher1 × Person2 has fields tid1 and id2.

Projection deduplicates. Selection does not.

Π returns a set, so Π[A,B] R on a six-tuple relation may have two tuples. This is not an optimisation, it is the definition — a relation is a set — and half the mechanics exercises below turn on remembering it. SQL's SELECT does not deduplicate, which is why the next set writes SELECT DISTINCT everywhere.

5.2Why we write the join out

Every exercise on this page is answered with σ, Π, ×, ∪, ∩, − and nothing else. That is a deliberate restriction, and it is the one the problem book imposes too.

The reason is that ⋈ hides the thing being marked. A join decides for itself which attributes to match on — every pair of columns that happen to share a name — so an answer written with a join never has to state the condition that links two relations. Writing Teacher1 × Department2 and then tid1 = chair2 forces you to know which foreign key you are following, and that is the entire content of a query answer. It is also what you will type into SQL in the next set, where the comma in the FROM clause is the product and the WHERE clause is your filter formula — there is no implicit matching there either.

Two consequences worth noticing before you start.

A product of relations that share an attribute name is still fine. The result simply has two columns of that name, which is what the numbered copies of §5.4 are for. A join would have silently merged them.

Over two relations with identical schemas, a join is intersection. R ⋈ S there matches on every attribute, so it keeps exactly the tuples both relations have — which is R ∩ S, an operation you are allowed. Exercises 5.3 and 5.5 use this.

5.3When an expression is impossible

Roughly a third of the mechanics exercises have no answer, and saying so precisely is the whole mark. There are exactly three ways an expression can fail to denote a relation.

faultwhere it biteshow to say it
not union compatible∪, ∩, −“R and T do not have identical relational schemas” — different arity, or the same arity with different attribute names or domains
unknown attribute in a filter formulaσ“the condition D = A is invalid, since attribute A is not in the schema of V”
unknown attribute in a projection listΠ“the schema T does not have attributes C and D”

Nothing else is impossible. In particular, an empty result is a perfectly good result. Give its schema and an empty body. Several exercises below are testing exactly this distinction, and “impossible” where an empty relation is wanted is a wrong answer.

5.4Numbered copies

There are no variables, so “a teacher who chairs two different departments” cannot be said directly. It is said by taking two copies of Department in one product and numbering them, so that the filter formula can mention one copy's field and not the other's:

Π[name2, salary1] σ[F] ( Teacher1 × Person2 × Department3 × Department4 )
    where   F  ≡  [ tid1 = id2  ∧  tid1 = chair3  ∧  tid1 = chair4  ∧  code3 ≠ code4 ]

Read it as: lay out every combination of one teacher, one person, and two departments; keep the combinations where the person is that teacher and both departments are chaired by them and the two departments are different; then report the name and salary. The final Π is doing real work — without it the same teacher appears once per qualifying pair of departments.

Three rules of thumb.

The inequality is what makes “two” mean two. Drop code3 ≠ code4 and every teacher who chairs one department qualifies, paired with itself. Put the inequality on the key of the relation being doubled, or on whichever part of the key the other conditions have not already pinned down; an inequality on a non-key field says something weaker and usually something wrong.

The number of copies is the number of things the English quantifies over. “A department that paid X more than it paid Y” needs five — one department, two paychecks and two professors.

Every copy needs a condition tying it to the others. With n relations in the product you want at least n−1 equalities. A missing one is the single most common error in this topic, and it is invisible in the result: the answer has the right columns and roughly the right number of rows, and is wrong. Count the equalities against the copies before you hand the answer in.

5.5Losers and winners

The algebra has no “not exists”. Every query whose English contains no, never, not, only or a superlative is built the same way, in three steps:

stepnamewhat it holds
1the losersthe identifiers that are disqualified, because the thing you want to deny does happen to them
2the winnersall the identifiers, minus the losers
3the resultput the winners back against the full relation to recover the other fields you were asked for

The lectures write it with named intermediate results and a left arrow, which is clearer than one nested expression and is what you should do on paper:

Fail       ←  Π[sid] σ[grade = 'F'] GradeSheet
NeverFail  ←  Π[sid] Student  −  Fail
Result     ←  Π[name1] σ[id1 = sid2] ( Person1 × NeverFail2 )

Step 1 is where the thinking is, and it is always the positive version of the question. “Students that never failed” → the losers are the students who did fail. “Departments that have never issued a paycheck” → the losers are the departments that have. Step 2 never varies. Step 3 is needed because the winners are bare identifiers and the question asked for names.

Both sides of a difference must be union compatible, which in practice means the losers must be projected onto exactly the key you are subtracting from — one column, matching domain. If your losers have two columns the difference will not typecheck, and the fix is to project harder.

Subtract from the right relation

Π[sid] Student − Fail, not Π[sid] GradeSheet − Fail. A student with no grade sheet at all has never failed anything and belongs in the answer, and only the first version finds them. The rule generalises: step 2 subtracts from the relation that lists everyone who could have qualified, which is almost never the relation the losers came out of.

A superlative is a negative

“The highest-paid teacher” has no maximum operator behind it. Turn it round: a teacher is disqualified from being highest-paid exactly when somebody earns more. So the losers are the teachers who are out-earned by somebody — a product of Teacher with itself, selected on salary1 < salary2, projected onto tid1. The winners are everybody else, and that is the maximum. Exercise 5.15 is this query; “the lowest”, “the oldest” and “the most” are the same move with the inequality turned around.

5.8Exercises: the College database

The seven queries worked in Lectures 8 and 9, against the schema of §5.6. Use only σ, Π, ×, ∪, ∩, −.

(a) Find Person born after 2005. (b) Find id of teachers where their salary is greater than $100,000.
(a)  σ[yearborn > 2005] Person

(b)  Π[tid] σ[F] Teacher
         where   F  ≡  [ salary > 100000 ]

The two smallest queries in the set, and each has exactly one thing in it.

(a) is a bare selection, so the schema of the answer is the whole of Person — id, name, address and yearborn. If the question had asked for the names, a Π[name] would go on the outside.

A note on the inequality. “Born after 2005” strictly means yearborn > 2005, which excludes anyone born in 2005 itself. The lecture writes ≥ 2005, which includes them. On our data that is the difference between five people and seven. Neither reading is wrong as English — the point is that the ambiguity is in the question, and a good answer states which reading it took. Exams are written by people, and “after”, “over” and “more than” are the three words most often meant loosely.

(b) shows the order that always applies: select first, then project. Reverse them and salary is gone before the filter can look at it. Note also that the answer is a list of tids and not of names — which is what was asked, but is almost never what is wanted; the next exercise is the version that fixes it.

Find the id and name of students who enroll after 2025.
Π[id2, name2] σ[F] ( Student1 × Person2 )
    where   F  ≡  [ sid1 = id2  ∧  yearenroll1 ≥ 2025 ]

The first query that needs two relations, and the template for every one that follows. The shape never changes: put in the product the relations you need, then write one equality per foreign-key link and one condition per adjective in the question.

Here the single link is sid1 = id2 and the single adjective is yearenroll1 ≥ 2025. Drop the link and you get every student paired with every person — a result that is not merely wrong but silently plausible, since it has the right columns and far too many rows.

Why Person has to be here at all. The question asks for a name, and Student has no name in it — the specialization put every shared attribute on the superclass. That is true of almost every query in this section: whenever the answer includes a name, an address or a birth year, Person appears in the product even though the English never mentions it.

Which copy to project. id2, not sid1. They are equal, so either gives the same values — but the question asked for the student's id and name as a pair from the person record, and keeping the projection list on one copy is the habit that stops you losing track in the four-relation queries below.

Find the name and salary of those teacher(s) that chair more than one department.
Π[name2, salary1] σ[F] ( Teacher1 × Person2 × Department3 × Department4 )
    where   F  ≡  [ tid1 = id2  ∧  tid1 = chair3  ∧  tid1 = chair4  ∧  code3 ≠ code4 ]

Four relations and four conditions. Build it by counting the nouns in the English: one teacher, whose name you need (so a person), and two departments. Then one equality per link — teacher to person, teacher to each department — and finally the one condition the question is actually about.

The inequality is what makes “more than one” mean more than one. Without code3 ≠ code4, copies 3 and 4 may be the same department and every chair qualifies, paired with themselves. The inequality goes on code because that is Department's key.

Doubling the right relation. Department, not Teacher — the repeated fact is a department, and the teacher is one person. Getting this backwards is the usual error in this family of queries, and the test is always the same: double whichever relation holds the thing there is more than one of.

Symmetry. Every qualifying pair of departments is found twice, once in each order, so the product's output is doubled before Π merges it. If the question had asked “how many such pairs”, the algebra could not have told you — and would have been out by a factor of two if it could.

Find the names of chairs of those departments in whose classes at least one student failed in year 2026.
Π[name1] σ[F] ( Person1 × Department2 × GradeSheet3 × Section4 )
    where   F  ≡  [ id1 = chair2  ∧  code2 = dcode4  ∧
                    reqNum3 = reqNum4  ∧  year3 = year4  ∧
                    grade3 = 'F'  ∧  year4 = 2026 ]

The longest chain so far, and every link in it is a foreign key you identified in Exercise 4.9. Read the filter formula as a path: a person who is the chair of a department, whose code appears on a section, which carries a grade sheet, on which the grade is F and the year is 2026.

The link from grade sheet to section is a pair. GradeSheet references Section on (reqNum, year), because that is Section's key — a registration number is reused from year to year. So two equalities are needed, not one, and omitting year3 = year4 matches a 2026 grade sheet against a 2025 section with the same registration number. That is a wrong answer that looks completely reasonable.

Why Course is not in the product. A section belongs to a department through its course, so you might expect Section → Course → Department. But Section carries dcode directly — it has to, as half of its composite foreign key — so the department code is already there and the hop through Course is unnecessary. Going through Course anyway is not wrong, just longer.

“At least one” needs nothing at all. The product supplies one tuple per failing grade sheet, and the final Π collapses them, so a department with five failures still reports its chair once. Existential quantification is free in this algebra; it is negation and universal quantification that cost you a difference.

Find name of all students whose name is also the name of another student.
Π[name1] σ[F] ( Person1 × Student2 × Person3 × Student4 )
    where   F  ≡  [ id1 = sid2  ∧  id3 = sid4  ∧  id1 ≠ id3  ∧  name1 = name3 ]

Two students, so two copies of both Person and Student — four relations for what the English describes as one comparison. Copies 1 and 2 are one student; copies 3 and 4 are the other.

The two conditions at the end do opposite jobs. id1 ≠ id3 says they are different people; name1 = name3 says they have the same name. Drop the inequality and every student matches themselves and the answer is every student's name. Drop the equality and the answer is every student's name again, for a different reason. Both are needed, and neither alone is close.

Why Student appears at all. Without copies 2 and 4, the query would find people who share a name, which includes teachers. The two Student copies are doing nothing but asserting that both people are students — a relation in a product purely as a membership test, with none of its attributes used in the filter formula or the projection. That is a perfectly ordinary thing to need, and it is worth being comfortable with it.

A wrinkle in the answer. The result is a set of names, so it cannot tell you which students they belonged to; and if three students shared a name, the name still appears once. Projecting onto id1, name1 instead would be more useful and is a fair thing to offer alongside the asked-for answer.

Find names of students that never failed a course.
Fail       ←  Π[sid] σ[grade = 'F'] GradeSheet
NeverFail  ←  Π[sid] Student  −  Fail
Result     ←  Π[name1] σ[id1 = sid2] ( Person1 × NeverFail2 )
The attempt that does not work 
Π[name1] σ[F] ( Person1 × GradeSheet2 )
    where   F  ≡  [ id1 = sid2  ∧  grade2 ≠ 'F' ]

This gives you the students who passed at least one course, which is a different set. A student who passed two courses and failed a third satisfies it, on the strength of either of the passes. The word is never: not one single failure, anywhere.

Why no selection can express it. A selection looks at one tuple at a time. “Never failed” is a claim about all of a student's grade sheets at once — and worse, about the absence of a tuple rather than the presence of one. No tuple of GradeSheet knows what the student's other grade sheets say. The only available move is to enumerate everyone who fails the test, which is expressible because failing needs only one bad grade, and subtract.

Subtract from Student, not from GradeSheet. A student with no grade sheet at all has never failed anything, and belongs in the answer. Π[sid] GradeSheet − Fail would miss them entirely, and on our data it misses exactly one student — which is the kind of error that survives testing.

And a wrinkle in the answer. Because the result is projected onto name, a name reappears if any student with that name qualifies. We have two students called John Chen; one never failed and one did, and the name is in the answer. Projecting onto id1, name1 gives the honest version. The question asked for names, so the answer above is correct — but it is worth a sentence, because the marker will be pleased and because in real life somebody would act on this list.

Find name and rank of the teacher(s) with highest salary.
Loser   ←  Π[tid1] σ[salary1 < salary2] ( Teacher1 × Teacher2 )
Winner  ←  Π[tid] Teacher  −  Loser
Result  ←  Π[name2, rank3] σ[F] ( Winner1 × Person2 × Teacher3 )
            where   F  ≡  [ tid1 = id2  ∧  tid1 = tid3 ]

A superlative, with no maximum operator to reach for. The move is the one in §5.5: a teacher is disqualified from being highest-paid exactly when somebody out-earns them, so the losers are the teachers for whom such a somebody exists — a product of Teacher with itself. The winners are everybody else.

The comparison must be strict. With salary1 ≤ salary2 every teacher is a loser against themselves, Winner comes back empty, and the query returns nothing. This is the most common way to get this query wrong and it fails silently — an empty answer looks like a data problem, not a logic one.

Ties are handled for free. If two teachers share the top salary, neither out-earns the other, so neither is a loser and both are returned. You did not have to ask for that, and in SQL you will have to think about it — ORDER BY salary LIMIT 1 would quietly return one.

Step 3 needs two relations, not one. Winner holds bare tids; the name lives on Person and the rank on Teacher, so both have to come back into the product. That is a consequence of the specialization mapping, and it is why this query is longer than the same question about the Payroll database, where one relation holds everything.

For the lowest salary instead, turn the inequality around: salary1 > salary2.

5.9Exercises: Professor Payroll

The same techniques against the problem book's database, where membership is M : M and the questions get longer. Same restriction: only σ, Π, ×, ∪, ∩, −.

(a) Find department name and amount of each issued paycheck. (b) Find department name and serial number of the paychecks whose amount is below 1,000. (c) Find full name and salary of each professor that serves in the rank of Chief Wizard.
(a)  Π[dpt, amount] paycheck

(b)  Π[dpt, serNum] σ[F] paycheck
         where   F  ≡  [ amount < 1000 ]

(c)  Π[firstName, lastName, salary] σ[F] professor
         where   F  ≡  [ rank = 'Chief Wizard' ]

Three one-relation queries, to get used to the schema.

(a) is a bare projection, and it is already lossy in an interesting way: two departments that happened to issue paychecks of the same amount collapse to one tuple, because Π deduplicates. Had the question asked “how much was paid out in total”, no expression in this algebra would answer it — there is no aggregation here at all.

(c) is a string comparison. Note that the whole rank is the value: rank = 'Chief' matches nothing, and there is no “starts with” in the algebra.

(a) Find names and phone numbers of those departments whose chair earns less than 1,000 in salary. (b) Find full names (first and last) of all professors who are members of the department named Potions. (c) Find full names of all professors who serve as department chairs.
(a)  Π[name1, phone1] σ[F] ( department1 × professor2 )
         where   F  ≡  [ chairId1 = socSecNum2  ∧  salary2 < 1000 ]

(b)  Π[firstName1, lastName1] σ[F] ( professor1 × member2 )
         where   F  ≡  [ socSecNum1 = profId2  ∧  deptName2 = 'Potions' ]

(c)  Π[firstName1, lastName1] σ[F] ( professor1 × department2 )
         where   F  ≡  [ socSecNum1 = chairId2 ]

The two ways a professor can be attached to a department, and they are not symmetrical. Membership lives in its own relation because it is M : M; chairing lives in a column of department because it is 1 : M. So (b) goes through member and (c) does not, and knowing which without looking it up is most of what Set 4 was for.

(c) has no condition beyond the link, which is worth noticing: “is a chair” is not a property stored on a professor, it is the existence of a matching tuple in department. The equality is the question.

Unlike the College database, professor carries its own names, so nothing here needs a second relation just to find out what someone is called. Compare Exercise 5.10, where it did.

(a) Find the full name of the chairs of those departments that have issued at least one paycheck in the amount higher than 10,000. (b) Find id, first name, and last name of those professors who have received paychecks from (at least) two different departments. (c) Find id, first name, and last name of those professors who have received at least two (different) paychecks from the same department on the same amount.
(a)  Π[firstName1, lastName1] σ[F] ( professor1 × department2 × paycheck3 )
         where   F  ≡  [ socSecNum1 = chairId2  ∧  name2 = dpt3  ∧  amount3 ≥ 10000 ]

(b)  Π[socSecNum1, firstName1, lastName1] σ[F] ( professor1 × paycheck2 × paycheck3 )
         where   F  ≡  [ socSecNum1 = prof2  ∧  socSecNum1 = prof3  ∧  dpt2 ≠ dpt3 ]

(c)  Π[socSecNum1, firstName1, lastName1] σ[F] ( professor1 × paycheck2 × paycheck3 )
         where   F  ≡  [ socSecNum1 = prof2  ∧
                         socSecNum1 = prof3  ∧
                         dpt2 = dpt3         ∧
                         serNum2 ≠ serNum3  ∧
                         amount2 = amount3 ]

(b) and (c) are the same product twice; only the last three conjuncts differ, and they are the whole exercise.

In (b) the inequality is on the department, because “two different departments” is the condition. In (c) it has to be on the serial number — the department is now required to be the same, and so is the amount, so the only thing left that can distinguish the two paychecks is the rest of the key. Since paycheck is keyed on (dpt, serNum) and the departments agree, serNum2 ≠ serNum3 is precisely “a different tuple”.

That is the general rule, worth stating in an answer: to say “two different X”, put the inequality on the key of X, or on whichever part of the key the other conditions have not already pinned down.

(a) Find department name and phone number of those departments that have issued a paycheck to Professor named Sybill Trelawney as well as to Professor named Firenze Centaur, but the amount paid on Professor Trelawney's check was higher than the amount paid on Professor Centaur's check. (b) Find full names of all professors who are members of more than one department.
(a)  Π[name1, phone1] σ[F] ( department1 × paycheck2 × paycheck3 × professor4 × professor5 )
         where   F  ≡  [ name1 = dpt2  ∧
                         name1 = dpt3  ∧
                         prof2 = socSecNum4  ∧
                         prof3 = socSecNum5  ∧
                         firstName4 = 'Sybill'  ∧  lastName4 = 'Trelawney'  ∧
                         firstName5 = 'Firenze' ∧  lastName5 = 'Centaur'    ∧
                         amount2 > amount3 ]

(b)  Π[firstName3, lastName3] σ[F] ( member1 × member2 × professor3 )
         where   F  ≡  [ profId1 = profId2  ∧  deptName1 ≠ deptName2  ∧
                         socSecNum3 = profId1 ]

(a) is the largest product on the page and it is built mechanically, by counting the nouns: one department, two paychecks, two professors — five copies. Then one equality per link: the department to each paycheck (two), each paycheck to its professor (two), which is four for five relations, the minimum. Everything after that is adjectives.

Writing it in that order — copies, then links, then adjectives — is the difference between a long answer and a wrong one. Attempting it in English order produces a tangle, and a missing link is never visible in the result.

(b) is Exercise 5.11's shape with member doubled instead of Department, because membership is where the multiplicity lives here — there is no column anywhere that holds a professor's departments.

(a) Find full name (first and last) of those professors who are members in some department where the chair earns less than 1,000 dollars in salary. (b) Find full name and rank of each professor that earns a higher salary than their department chair.
(a)  Π[firstName1, lastName1] σ[F] ( professor1 × member2 × department3 × professor4 )
         where   F  ≡  [ socSecNum1 = profId2  ∧
                         deptName2 = name3  ∧
                         chairId3 = socSecNum4  ∧
                         salary4 < 1000 ]

(b)  Π[firstName1, lastName1, rank1] σ[F] ( professor1 × professor2 × member3 × department4 )
         where   F  ≡  [ socSecNum1 = profId3  ∧
                         deptName3 = name4  ∧
                         chairId4 = socSecNum2  ∧
                         salary1 > salary2 ]

Both need professor twice, and the two copies mean different people: copy 1 is the member being reported, the other is the chair being tested. They are joined through a chain of three equalities — member to department, department to chair — and if you lose track of which copy is which, the query silently answers a different question. In (a), merging copies 1 and 4 asks for professors whose own salary is below 1,000.

Naming the copies as you introduce them helps: professor1 “the member”, professor4 “the chair”. The problem book does not do this, but nothing stops you, and it costs nothing in an exam.

In (b) the word “their” is doing all the work. It ties the chair to the professor's own department, and that tie is the three-equality chain. Break any link and the query becomes “professors who earn more than some chair somewhere”, which is a much weaker claim and returns far too many tuples.

A professor in two departments is tested against each chair separately and qualifies if either comparison holds, since the product produces one tuple per membership and Π merges them. That is almost certainly the intended reading, but it is worth a sentence under your answer, because the question does not settle it.

(a) Find the full name and rank of those department chairs that have never received a paycheck from any department which they chair (though they may have been paid by other departments). (b) Find names of all departments that have no members. (c) Find department name and phone number of those departments that have never issued a paycheck.
(a)  Loser   ←  Π[prof1] σ[F] ( paycheck1 × department2 )
                 where   F  ≡  [ prof1 = chairId2  ∧  dpt1 = name2 ]
     Winner  ←  Π[chairId] department  −  Loser
     Result  ←  Π[firstName1, lastName1, rank1] σ[G] ( professor1 × Winner2 )
                 where   G  ≡  [ socSecNum1 = socSecNum2 ]

(b)  Π[name] department  −  Π[deptName] member

(c)  Loser   ←  Π[dpt] paycheck
     Winner  ←  Π[name] department  −  Loser
     Result  ←  Π[name1, phone1] σ[F] ( department1 × Winner2 )
                 where   F  ≡  [ name1 = name2 ]

Three negatives, in increasing order of effort.

(b) is the pattern at its smallest: both sides are projections onto a single column of the same domain, so the difference is the answer and no step 3 is needed. Note why the schemas match despite the different attribute names — union compatibility is about arity and domains, and name and deptName both range over department names.

(c) adds step 3. The winners hold bare department names, but the question asked for phone numbers too, so they are put back against department to pick up the rest of the tuple.

(a) is the one that rewards care, because the losers are not simply “chairs who were paid”. A chair paid by a department they do not chair is still a winner — the parenthesis in the question says so — so the loser formula needs both equalities: the paycheck's professor is the chair, and the paycheck's department is the one being chaired. Drop dpt1 = name2 and the answer quietly becomes “chairs who have never been paid at all”.

Note also that the losers are projected onto one column, prof, so that they are union compatible with Π[chairId] department. Projecting onto the pair (prof, dpt) would have been natural to write and would not have typechecked.

(a) Find name and phone number of those departments whose chair is not a member in the department. (b) Find full name and rank of each professor that is not a member in the department named Potions. (c) Find full names (first and last) of all professors who are not members of any department where Professor Dolores Umbridge is a member.
(a)  Loser   ←  Π[name1] σ[F] ( department1 × member2 )
                 where   F  ≡  [ name1 = deptName2  ∧  profId2 = chairId1 ]
     Winner  ←  Π[name] department  −  Loser
     Result  ←  Π[name1, phone1] σ[G] ( department1 × Winner2 )
                 where   G  ≡  [ name1 = name2 ]

(b)  Loser   ←  Π[profId] σ[deptName = 'Potions'] member
     Winner  ←  Π[socSecNum] professor  −  Loser
     Result  ←  Π[firstName2, lastName2, rank2] σ[G] ( Winner1 × professor2 )
                 where   G  ≡  [ socSecNum1 = socSecNum2 ]

(c)  Loser   ←  Π[profId1] σ[F] ( member1 × member2 × professor3 )
                 where   F  ≡  [ deptName1 = deptName2  ∧
                                 socSecNum3 = profId2  ∧
                                 firstName3 = 'Dolores'  ∧  lastName3 = 'Umbridge' ]
     Winner  ←  Π[socSecNum] professor  −  Loser
     Result  ←  Π[firstName2, lastName2] σ[G] ( Winner1 × professor2 )
                 where   G  ≡  [ socSecNum1 = socSecNum2 ]

(b) is the bare pattern with a selection inside the loser formula — and note that the selection goes inside, before the projection, because deptName is needed to apply it and gone afterwards.

(c) is the hardest negative on the page, because the thing being denied is itself a two-relation condition. The losers are “professors who share some department with Umbridge”, which needs two copies of member: copy 2 is Umbridge's membership, copy 1 is the candidate's, and deptName1 = deptName2 is the sharing.

Why the difference has to come last. The English is “not a member of any such department” — a universal negative. No tuple of member knows about the professor's other memberships, so it cannot be expressed by selecting tuples. The only available move is to enumerate everyone who fails the test, which is expressible since failing needs only one shared department, and subtract. An attempt at σ[deptName1 ≠ deptName2] answers a different and much weaker question: professors who have a department Umbridge is not in.

Umbridge herself shares every one of her departments with herself, so she lands among the losers and is excluded. That is the right reading of the English and worth a sentence, since the question does not say. And if Umbridge belonged to no department, the losers would be empty and the answer every professor — also correct, and a useful sanity check on the shape: a vacuous universal is true.

Going further

Two things the algebra on this page cannot do. It cannot count, sum or average — there is no aggregation operation, so “the total payroll of each department” and “how many courses did this student fail” have no expression here at all. And it cannot ask for a transitive closure: “everyone in the chain of command above this person” needs recursion, which is where Datalog comes in.

What it can do, it does with five operations and no quantifiers, and that economy is why it is the thing query optimisers actually manipulate. Set 6 translates these queries into SQL and runs them, and the first surprise there is how little the translation changes — a product becomes a comma, a selection becomes a WHERE, a projection becomes the SELECT list, and the losers-and-winners pattern becomes a subquery you will recognise immediately.
